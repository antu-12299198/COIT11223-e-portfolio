## Summary of the Artefact
This ABC News article discusses the dispute between Australia’s eSafety Commissioner and X over graphic videos of the Sydney church stabbing. The Commissioner asked X to remove the content worldwide, but X challenged the order. Although the legal action was later dropped, the case raised an important question about how much power Australian authorities should have over content published on global social media platforms (Evans & Butler 2024).

## Justification on the Artefact
I chose this artefact because it connects closely with the Week 9 topic of government censorship and internet regulation. Before reading about this case, I had not really thought about how difficult it can be for one country to control content on a global platform. This case helped me see both sides of the issue. Removing harmful content can help protect people online, but it can also raise concerns about freedom of expression and how much control governments should have. In my view, governments need to find a balance between protecting people from harmful online content and respecting individual freedom.

## Summary of the Artefact
This Reuters article discusses Nepal’s decision to lift its social media ban after large protests across the country. The government had blocked several social media platforms because they did not follow registration requirements. However, the ban became a major concern for young protesters, along with wider issues such as corruption and limited economic opportunities. After the protests led to deadly unrest, the government decided to remove the social media restrictions (Reuters 2025).


## Justification on the Artefact
I selected this artefact because it shows how government control of digital platforms can affect people beyond just the internet. What stood out to me was how quickly the social media restrictions became part of wider public dissatisfaction. Before learning about this topic, I mostly thought censorship involved removing certain posts or blocking websites. This example helped me understand that governments can also restrict access to entire social media platforms. It made me realise that decisions about online censorship can have serious social and political impacts, especially because many people depend on social media for communication, news and sharing information.

# Artefact 3: Australia's Under-16 Social Media Restrictions

https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/australia-makes-history-with-the-social-media-minimum-age

---

## Summary of the Artefact
This eSafety Commissioner resource explains Australia’s minimum-age rules for social media. From 10 December 2025, age-restricted social media platforms must take reasonable steps to stop Australians under the age of 16 from creating or keeping accounts. The aim of this measure is to better protect young people from potential harms linked to social media use and persuasive features used by online platforms (eSafety Commissioner 2025).

## Justification on the Artefact
I chose this artefact because it is a current Australian example of how governments regulate access to online platforms. I found it interesting because there are arguments on both sides. The policy is designed to protect younger users from possible online harms, but at the same time, it limits their ability to have accounts on certain social media platforms. This helped me better understand the ethical question discussed in Week 9 about where the line should be between protecting people online and restricting their freedom. I learned that censorship and internet regulation are not always simple issues. Governments and ICT professionals need to think carefully about safety, individual rights, whether the rules actually work, and any unexpected consequences they may create.
