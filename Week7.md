## Summary of the Artefact
This WIPO factsheet explores the intellectual property risks created by generative AI. AI tools can produce text, images, code, audio and video, but they are often trained using large amounts of data that may include copyrighted material. This creates uncertainty about copyright infringement and ownership of AI-generated content. WIPO suggests that organisations should use properly licensed data, review AI tools carefully and introduce policies to reduce intellectual property risks (WIPO 2024, pp. 3, 7–8).

## Justification on the Artefact
I chose this artefact because it helped me understand that using generative AI involves more than simply creating content. Before reading it, I did not think much about where AI training data came from or who owned AI-generated work. Afterwards, I realised that AI can create copyright problems for both developers and users. This made me understand why ICT professionals need to respect creators' rights and check how AI-generated content is produced before using it (WIPO 2024, pp. 11–12).65

## Summary of the Artefact
This peer-reviewed article investigates how internet piracy affects book sales. The researchers conducted a year-long experiment where unauthorised online copies of selected books were removed, while other books were left unprotected. The removal process successfully reduced the availability of pirated copies. The traditional analysis found only a small positive effect on sales, while the Bayesian analysis suggested that protecting books from piracy increased sales by about 9% (Hardy, Krawczyk & Tyrowicz 2024, pp. 457–458).

## Justification on the Artefact
I chose this artefact because it helped me understand the real impact of digital piracy on creators and businesses. Before reading the article, I knew piracy was illegal but did not think much about its financial effects. Afterwards, I realised that reducing unauthorised sharing can potentially improve legitimate sales. It showed me why copyright protection is important for protecting creators' work while encouraging people to access digital content through legal sources (Hardy, Krawczyk & Tyrowicz 2024, pp. 468–469).

## Summary of the Artefact
This WIPO video explains how intellectual property helps protect the ideas and creations of innovators, artists and businesses. It shows that IP rights allow creators to benefit from their work and encourage them to continue creating and investing in new ideas. The video also discusses copyright, patents and trademarks and explains how they support innovation around the world. It emphasises that intellectual property should remain human-centred as technologies such as AI continue to develop (WIPO 2025).

## Justification on the Artefact
I chose this video because it helped me understand why intellectual property is important beyond simply protecting ownership. Before watching it, I mainly thought IP was about stopping people from copying someone else's work. Afterwards, I realised that protecting intellectual property also encourages creativity and gives creators the opportunity to benefit from their effort. The discussion about AI was particularly interesting because it showed me that technology is changing how we think about creators and ownership. This made me realise that ICT professionals should respect intellectual property while supporting new technology and innovation (WIPO 2025).

## Summary of the Artefact
The key idea that stood out to me in the Week 7 workshop was the Monkey Selfie Copyright Dispute. A macaque named Naruto took photographs using photographer David Slater's camera, creating a debate about who owned the copyright. Wikimedia considered the photographs public domain because copyright requires human authorship, while Slater argued that his preparation made the photographs possible. The case raised interesting questions about authorship that are now also relevant to AI-generated content (COIT11223 Workshop Week 7, 2026).

## Justification on the Artefact
I chose this workshop idea because I found the case unusual but strongly connected to modern ICT. Before the discussion, I assumed that the owner of a camera would automatically own photographs taken with it. Afterwards, I realised copyright depends on authorship rather than simply owning the technology. It also made me think about AI-generated images because AI cannot currently own copyright. This discussion helped me understand how new technologies can create ethical questions that existing copyright laws may not clearly address (COIT11223 Workshop Week 7, 2026).
